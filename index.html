The science behind the experience

Why can a false memory feel real?

The Mandela Effect is a popular term for a shared false memory: multiple people report remembering a detail in the same way, even though that recollection conflicts with the available record. The term describes the experience; it is not, by itself, a scientific explanation.

Reconstructive memoryRemembering is not like replaying a perfect recording. People reconstruct events using fragments, expectations, knowledge, and cues available at the time of retrieval.
Source-monitoring errorsSometimes we remember information but misattribute where it came from—for example, confusing an image we imagined, saw in a parody, or heard from someone else with the original source.
Confidence is not the same as accuracyConfidence is a judgment about memory, not a direct measurement of whether the memory is correct. Familiarity and repeated exposure can make a detail feel compelling.
Important limitation: This small demonstration cannot establish why a particular Mandela Effect occurs, prove that a false memory is universal, or diagnose an individual's memory. The item set is a convenience sample of familiar cultural examples.

A question your group can test

Research question: How closely does confidence track recognition accuracy on familiar Mandela Effect examples?

Measurable outcomes: proportion correct, proportion of lure selections, mean confidence, and the difference between mean confidence and accuracy. You can examine whether confidence is higher on correct than incorrect trials.
